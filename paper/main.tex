\begin{abstract}
Next-Concept Modeling (NCM) generates text coarse to fine: a multiscale residual VQ-VAE turns a sequence into twelve levels of discrete codes, and a transformer predicts one level per pass. We find that the code stacks NCM samples are not the stacks its own tokenizer assigns to the text they decode to: re-encoding a sample rewrites 61--96\% of the codes at each of the six coarsest levels, yet decodes back to the same text for 98\% of samples. We propose \emph{round-trip refinement}, a training-free sampler: decode the draft, re-encode the text, keep the re-encoded coarse levels, and regenerate the fine levels with the unchanged generator. On the released TinyStories and LM1B checkpoints, with the authors' own sampling and evaluation code, one round lowers GPT-2 Large generative perplexity by 17\% and 22\% at equal unigram entropy for six extra passes; two or three rounds reach $-31\%$ and $-29\%$. Resampling the fine levels without re-encoding gives nothing, and re-quantizing the latent instead of the text recovers at most half. The gain is not temperature in disguise: at matched corpus diversity refinement sits 10--15\% below the temperature frontier, exact 11-pass twins without the round trip gain nothing, and it does not sharpen real human text. On TinyStories, how non-canonical a draft is predicts its quality (Spearman 0.37) and the round trip helps most where the rewrite is largest, while selecting drafts by canonicality only trades diversity for perplexity. Paired with a per-level temperature schedule (schedule plus round trip), the samplers are about 21\% more fluent than the released one at equal or higher diversity. Everything replicates on held-out seeds, three model sizes and a second scorer; an LLM judge prefers refined stories (0.63) and MAUVE rises from 0.88 to 0.94--0.96. Refinement gives small gains on hard Sudoku and XSum summaries, none on WMT14 translation or on the VAR image model, whose tokenizer round trips are lossy; seven training-side ideas, including distilling the refined sampler back into the generator, did not beat it at matched diversity. Code: \url{https://github.com/PierreKhoury1/ncm-round-trip}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Diffusion language models generate in parallel but need many denoising steps, each over the full sequence \citep{sahoo2024mdlm,wang2025remdm}. Next-Concept Modeling \citep[NCM;][]{kumbong2026ncm} takes a different route, borrowed from next-scale prediction in vision \citep{tian2024var,kumbong2025hmar}: a multiscale residual VQ-VAE encodes a 128-token sequence into $K{=}12$ levels of codes, from one code for the whole sequence to 128 codes at the finest level, and a transformer generates the levels coarse to fine, all codes of a level in parallel. NCM reaches the generative perplexity of strong diffusion baselines with an order of magnitude fewer function evaluations (NFE).

Each NCM level is sampled conditioned on the generator's own samples of the coarser levels. In training, by contrast, the coarser levels are the tokenizer's codes for real text, perturbed by uniform code replacement \citep[``conditioning corruption'';][\S3.4]{kumbong2026ncm}. Residual quantization is many-to-one: many code stacks decode to the same tokens, and the tokenizer always produces one particular stack, the greedy residual quantization of its encoder output. A sampled stack need not be that one. We measure this directly: decoding a generated sample and re-encoding the text changes 61--96\% of the codes at each of the six coarsest levels (20--72\% at the finer ones), yet decoding the re-encoded stack gives back exactly the same text for 98\% of samples (Section~\ref{sec:why}).

This suggests a simple repair. Once a draft exists, the tokenizer can tell us how \emph{it} would have encoded that text. We keep the re-encoded coarse levels, which now form a stack of the kind the generator saw in training, and let the generator redo the fine levels on top of them (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). The procedure needs no training, no new parameters and no change to the authors' sampler or decoder; it reuses the released generator, tokenizer and evaluation scripts as they are.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A diagnosis: NCM's sampled code stacks are non-canonical (not the stacks the tokenizer assigns to their own decoded text), and re-encoding replaces them by canonical ones without changing the text (Section~\ref{sec:why}). On TinyStories, how non-canonical a draft is predicts how bad it is (Spearman 0.37 with its Gen.\ PPL), and the round trip helps most where the rewrite is largest.
\item Round-trip refinement, a training-free sampler with one knob, the restart level $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and ablations showing that the re-encoding through text, not the extra passes, carries the gain; plus a free per-sample gate on the same signal that spends rounds only where the draft drifts (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).
\item An evaluation on nine released checkpoints under the authors' protocols, with the temperature frontier, three diversity measures and exact 11-pass temperature twins as controls (Section~\ref{sec:results}): $-17\%$ to $-31\%$ gen-PPL on TinyStories and $-22\%$ to $-29\%$ on LM1B large, 10--15\% beyond the temperature frontier, and clear failure cases (translation with a lossy tokenizer, easy Sudoku).
\item Evidence beyond Gen.\ PPL: held-out seeds, an LLM judge, MAUVE against human text, a trend over three model sizes, and a reasoning task (Sudoku) where refinement fixes puzzles without breaking solved ones (Sections~\ref{sec:heldout}--\ref{sec:sudoku}).
\item Negative results for seven training-side ideas, including the two the NCM paper lists as future work and two pilots at the paper's batch size, so that others need not repeat them (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background: Next-Concept Modeling}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Tokenizer.} An encoder $E$ maps a token sequence $x \in \mathcal{V}^{L}$ ($L{=}128$) to features $z \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$. A multiscale residual quantizer with level lengths $\ell_0,\dots,\ell_{K-1}$ (for TinyStories $1,8,16,24,32,40,52,64,76,92,108,128$) then picks codes greedily,
\begin{equation}
r_k = \arg\min_{j} \big\| \mathrm{Down}_{\ell_k}(z - \hat z_{k-1}) - e_{k,j} \big\|, \qquad \hat z_k = \hat z_{k-1} + \phi_k\!\big(\mathrm{Up}_L(e_{k,r_k})\big),
\label{eq:rq}
\end{equation}
with codebooks $e_k$ of 4{,}096 entries and $\hat z_{-1}{=}0$. Read left to right: $z - \hat z_{k-1}$ is the \emph{residual}, the part of the features that the coarser levels $0..k{-}1$ have not yet explained; $\mathrm{Down}_{\ell_k}$ shrinks it from $L$ positions to the $\ell_k$ positions of level $k$; the $\arg\min$ picks, at each of those positions, the nearest entry $e_{k,j}$ of level $k$'s codebook, and its index $r_k \in \{0,\dots,4095\}^{\ell_k}$ is the level-$k$ code; $\mathrm{Up}_L$ stretches the chosen entries back to $L$ positions, a per-level projection $\phi_k$ maps them into feature space, and the result is added to the running reconstruction $\hat z_k$. Level 0 has one position ($\ell_0{=}1$), so $r_0$ is a single code for the whole sequence; level 11 has $\ell_{11}{=}L{=}128$, one code per token. A decoder $D$ maps the final reconstruction $\hat z_{K-1}$ back to token logits. We write $Q(E(x))$ for the full code stack of $x$ and $\mathrm{dec}(r)$ for decoding a stack to tokens with the authors' token picker (argmax, with frequency and adjacency penalties on LM1B).

\paragraph{Generator.} The NCM transformer predicts all $\ell_k$ codes of level $k$ in one pass from the reconstruction $\hat z_{k-1}$ resized to $\ell_k$ positions, with block-diagonal attention so that pass $k$ processes only $\ell_k$ positions. Unconditional generation draws $r_0$ from a unigram prior and then runs $K{-}1{=}11$ passes. Training uses teacher forcing on $Q(E(x))$ for real text, with a share of the input codes replaced uniformly at random (mean rate 0.5, decreasing with level), while targets stay clean.

\section{Round-Trip Refinement}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Round-trip refinement with restart levels $k_1, \dots, k_m$}
\label{alg:rt}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $r \gets \textsc{NCM-Sample}()$ \Comment{draft: $r_0$ from the prior, then $K{-}1$ passes}
\State $x \gets \mathrm{dec}(r)$
\For{$k \in (k_1, \dots, k_m)$}
  \State $r' \gets Q(E(x))$ \Comment{re-encode the current text}
  \State $r \gets \textsc{NCM-Sample}(\text{levels } 0..k{-}1 \text{ fixed to } r'_{0..k-1})$ \Comment{$K{-}k$ passes}
  \State $x \gets \mathrm{dec}(r)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $x$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:rt} gives the sampler. One round with restart level $k$, written $\RT{k}$, costs $K{-}k$ extra generator passes plus one tokenizer encoder pass. Multiple rounds are written $\RT{5}{\times}2$ (two rounds from level 5) or $\RT{3}{+}\RT{6}{+}\RT{9}$ (three rounds from successively finer levels). Because pass $k$ processes $\ell_k$ positions, regenerating levels $6..11$ re-processes 520 of the 641 positions of a full sample; FLOPs therefore grow by about $1.8\times$ for $\RT{6}$ and $3.3\times$ for three rounds, somewhat more than the NFE ratios of $1.5\times$ and $2.6\times$ suggest; measured wall-clock follows the FLOPs ($1.9\times$ and $3.8\times$ on one GPU, Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

\paragraph{Implementation.} We do not reimplement sampling. The authors' \texttt{generate} calls a per-level sampling function once per level; we wrap that function so that, for levels below $k$, it returns the re-encoded codes instead of sampling (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}). Code embeddings, level encodings, heads, attention masks and the token picker are therefore exactly the released ones. As a check, forcing \emph{every} level to the re-encoded stack reproduces the draft text exactly on TinyStories (8/8 samples tested), 27/32 on XSum and only 15/32 on WMT14, whose tokenizer round trip is lossy (Section~\ref{sec:cond}).

\paragraph{Ablations.} Two controls isolate where the gain comes from. \emph{Resample}$_k$ keeps the draft's own codes for levels below $k$ and regenerates the rest; it costs the same as $\RT{k}$ but involves no re-encoding, so it draws the fine levels from the same conditional distribution as the draft did. \emph{LatentRQ}$_k$ re-quantizes the draft's latent $\hat z_{K-1}$ with Eq.~\eqref{eq:rq} instead of decoding and re-encoding the text; it is the text analogue of scale-travel in DiverseVAR \citep{park2025diversevar}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Checkpoints and sampling.} We use the released NCM checkpoints for TinyStories \citep{eldan2023tinystories} (88.6M generator), LM1B \citep{chelba2013lm1b} small, medium and large, XSum \citep{narayan2018xsum} and WMT14 de--en \citep{bojar2014wmt}, with the released evaluation configurations: temperature 1, no top-$k$/top-$p$ and no guidance for TinyStories and LM1B (LM1B with the configured frequency and adjacency penalties in the token picker), greedy decoding for XSum and WMT14. Refinement rounds use the same settings as the draft unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{Metrics.} For TinyStories we report generative perplexity (Gen.\ PPL) under GPT-2 Large \citep{radford2019gpt2} and the mean per-sample unigram entropy $H$ of GPT-2 tokens, as in the NCM paper; for LM1B we use the authors' FLM protocol (corpus perplexity over the first 128 GPT-2 tokens of the decoded ids, entropy over raw ids). Because Gen.\ PPL can be lowered by reducing diversity \citep{pynadath2026frontiers,franca2026hacking}, we add corpus distinct-3 (unique word trigrams over all trigrams), cross-sample 4-gram overlap (xs-4, share of a sample's 4-grams that occur in another sample; lower is more diverse), and the rate of adjacent doubled words, and we compare every sampler with the \emph{temperature frontier}: the released sampler at $\tau \in \{1, 0.95, 0.9, 0.85, 0.8, 0.7\}$, interpolated at the same distinct-3 (TinyStories) or entropy (LM1B). XSum is scored with ROUGE-1/2/L \citep{lin2004rouge} plus GPT-2 Gen.\ PPL of the summaries, WMT14 with sacreBLEU \citep{post2018sacrebleu} and chrF. Unconditional results average three seeds of 256 samples each. All samplers of a seed share their level-0 draws; drafts are identical across samplers only for the first batch of each seed (samples 0--127 on TinyStories, 0--31 on LM1B), because refinement rounds consume random numbers before the second batch. Conditional results use the first 1{,}000 test documents.

\paragraph{Reproduction.} Our pipeline gives $16.15 \pm 0.17$ Gen.\ PPL for the released TinyStories sampler (seeds 1--3) and $16.32$ on seeds 4--6; the closest number in the NCM paper is 15.41, from its block-diagonal attention ablation, which is not the released evaluation run. All comparisons below are against our own reproduction, under identical code and seeds.

\paragraph{Main result.} Table~\ref{tab:ts} and Figure~\ref{fig:tsfrontier} give the main result. The released sampler scores 16.15 Gen.\ PPL and human-written stories score 8.51, so the released model sits 7.6 points above human text. One round from level 6 ($\RT{6}$) lowers Gen.\ PPL to 13.36, a 17.3\% drop that holds on every seed, while unigram entropy stays at 3.974 against 3.980: the stories read more fluently to GPT-2 Large, and the words they use are spread as widely as before. The restart level is the only knob. Restarting from a coarser level regenerates more of the story, and this helps up to $k{=}4$--$5$, where the gain reaches 20\%; restarting from level 3 rewrites too much, the draft's content starts to drift, and the gain falls back to 18\% while diversity drops (distinct-3 0.720 against 0.740 for $\RT{6}$). Further rounds keep improving: $\RT{5}{\times}2$ reaches 11.70 ($-27.6\%$) and $\RT{3}{+}\RT{6}{+}\RT{9}$ reaches 11.17 ($-30.9\%$). The latter closes 65\% of the 7.6-point gap between the released sampler and human text, at the price of 29 generator passes instead of 11.

\paragraph{Not a temperature effect.} Lowering the temperature also lowers Gen.\ PPL: $\tau{=}0.8$ gives 13.34, the same number as $\RT{6}$, so perplexity alone cannot tell the two apart. The diversity columns can. Temperature reaches 13.34 by making the stories more alike: distinct-3 falls from 0.749 to 0.719, and the share of a story's 4-grams that also occur in another story (xs-4) rises from 0.162 to 0.191. $\RT{6}$ keeps distinct-3 at 0.740 and xs-4 at 0.170, close to the released sampler. To compare the two fairly we interpolate the temperature curve ($\tau$ from 0.7 to 1.2) at each sampler's own distinct-3 and ask how much lower its perplexity is than temperature would give at that diversity (last column of Table~\ref{tab:ts}). By that measure single rounds are 6--12\% better than temperature for $k{=}5..8$ and multiple rounds 10--14\%; sampling levels 6--11 at $\tau{=}0.8$ in both the draft and the round ($\tau_{\geq 6}{=}0.8$) gives the largest single-round margin, 14.4\%. Refinement also removes a kind of error that temperature does not touch. Adjacent doubled words (``to to'', ``quickly quickly'') are a typical failure of predicting a whole level in parallel, since neighbouring positions cannot see each other's choice; they fall from 0.39\% of words to 0.23\% under $\RT{6}$, while every temperature setting leaves them at 0.32--0.36\%. The re-encoded coarse levels therefore change \emph{what} the fine levels produce, rather than only sharpening the same distribution.

\paragraph{A second domain and three model sizes.} The result carries over to LM1B, which differs from TinyStories in domain (news sentences rather than children's stories), tokenizer (BERT word pieces) and evaluation protocol (the authors' FLM scoring; Table~\ref{tab:lm1b}, Figure~\ref{fig:lm1b}). Perplexities are higher here because news sentences are harder to predict than children's stories, so the relative changes are what to read. On the small model one round lowers Gen.\ PPL by 11--18\% depending on the restart level, on the large model by 6--22\%, on every seed and at equal or slightly \emph{higher} entropy. Temperature cannot do this: $\tau{=}0.8$ reaches $-16\%$ on the large model only by dropping entropy from 4.339 to 4.286, i.e.\ by narrowing the vocabulary the samples draw from, whereas $\RT{6}$ reaches $-22\%$ at entropy 4.338. Two rounds from level 6 reach 56.63 on the large model ($-29.3\%$) at entropy 4.346, higher than the released sampler's. The released medium model behaves the same way ($\RT{6}$ $-20.6\%$, $\RT{6}{\times}2$ $-28.8\%$ at entropy 4.342 vs.\ 4.339). Across the three sizes the gain of $\RT{6}$ grows rather than shrinks, $-14.8\%$ (small), $-20.6\%$ (medium), $-22.0\%$ (large); if the round trip were only compensating for a weak generator, a stronger generator should have less to gain, so this ordering suggests the mismatch it repairs is a property of the training recipe, not of model capacity. Entropy is the least sensitive diversity axis on LM1B, so we also check the stricter one: measured on distinct-3 of the raw ids, $\RT{6}$ is 13--17\% and $\RT{6}{\times}2$ 17--21\% below the temperature curve on every seed (1--6), and the hot-draft sampler of Section~\ref{sec:hot} reaches $-21\%$ at the released sampler's own distinct-3 (0.894 vs.\ 0.896).

\paragraph{Summarisation gains, translation does not.} The two conditional tasks behave differently, and the difference is informative (Table~\ref{tab:cond}). On XSum, ROUGE measures overlap with the reference summary and Gen.\ PPL measures how fluent the summary itself reads. One round from level 3 raises ROUGE-1/2/L by $+0.25/+0.25/+0.33$, a small but consistent gain in content overlap, and lowers the Gen.\ PPL of the summaries from 82.4 to 69.5 ($-16\%$): the summaries say roughly the same things, but read noticeably better. Two rounds overshoot and lose both (ROUGE-L 27.59, Gen.\ PPL back at 82.5), so for a conditional task one round is the useful setting. On WMT14 de--en, by contrast, refinement lowers BLEU at every restart level, least for the coarsest restart ($\RT{4}$: 20.45 vs.\ 20.51, a wash) and by over 4 points for $\RT{6}$. The sanity check of Section~\ref{sec:method} explains why: re-encoding a WMT14 output and decoding it again, with no regeneration at all, already changes 17 of 32 outputs, so on this checkpoint the tokenizer cannot reproduce its own text. The round trip then injects wording changes before the generator even runs, and in translation exact wording is what BLEU rewards. Round-trip refinement therefore needs a tokenizer whose round trip is (near-)lossless on generated text; on TinyStories it is exact, on XSum nearly so (27/32), and on WMT14 it is not.

\subsection{Why the round trip helps}
\label{sec:why}

\paragraph{Sampled stacks are non-canonical.} Figure~\ref{fig:changed} shows, for 768 TinyStories drafts, the share of codes per level that change when the draft's stack is replaced by the tokenizer's stack for its decoded text. The disagreement is largest where it matters most: 96\% of the single level-0 codes change, i.e.\ the tokenizer almost never picks the ``whole-story'' code the generator started from; 61--84\% change at levels 1--5, the levels that fix the content; and 20--72\% at the finer levels, which spell out the words. Yet decoding the re-encoded stack reproduces 751 of the 768 drafts exactly (97.8\% of drafts, 99.98\% of tokens). So the generator's stack and the tokenizer's stack are two different descriptions of the same text, and the difference is invisible in the output. It is not invisible to the generator: in training, its inputs were always tokenizer stacks (with uniform noise), so at sampling time its fine levels are conditioned on coarse codes of a kind it has never seen. This is the train--test mismatch the round trip removes.

\paragraph{Non-canonicality predicts quality.} If the mismatch is what hurts, drafts whose coarse codes the tokenizer rewrites most should be the worst drafts, and the ones the round trip helps most. Table~\ref{tab:canon} tests this per sample: for each draft we measure its \emph{non-canonicality}, the share of its level 0--5 codes that re-encoding changes, and its own GPT-2 Large perplexity. For the released sampler the two are rank-correlated at $\rho{=}0.37$ ($\pm0.12$ across seeds; $p<10^{-4}$ on every seed), a moderate correlation for a per-sample signal, and it is large enough to matter in practice: the fifth of drafts rewritten least has Gen.\ PPL 13.6, the fifth rewritten most 20.1, a 48\% spread in quality predicted by the tokenizer alone. $\RT{6}$ lowers the perplexity of 92\% of individual stories, so the average gains above are not carried by a few samples, and its per-story gain is itself correlated with non-canonicality ($\rho{=}0.28$): the least-rewritten fifth moves from 13.6 to 11.9, the most-rewritten from 20.1 to 15.1, i.e.\ the worst drafts gain almost three times as much as the best. On TinyStories, non-canonicality is thus a free, tokenizer-side quality signal for a draft, available before any reference model is consulted, and the drafts it flags are the ones the round trip repairs most. On LM1B large the signal is weak, and the reason is visible in the numbers: nearly every draft is rewritten 85--97\% (mean 0.86 against 0.76 on TinyStories, where drafts range from 0.46 to 0.90), so there is little variation between drafts for a correlation to pick up. The rank correlation is only $0.10 \pm 0.09$ and the fifths' draft perplexities rise only from 76.4 to 84.7; $\RT{6}$ still improves 88\% of sequences, by amounts that barely depend on the score ($\rho{=}0.09$). The score separates drafts where non-canonicality varies between them, not where it is uniformly high; the repair works in both cases.

\paragraph{Selecting drafts by canonicality is a diversity trade.} The signal suggests a selector: draw $N$ drafts and keep, per sample, the one re-encoding changes least (Table~\ref{tab:bestof}). At first sight it works: best-of-8 lowers Gen.\ PPL by 12.9\%, almost as much as $\RT{6}$ ($-17.3\%$), although for $8\times$ the passes. The diversity columns show what it actually does. By picking the drafts the tokenizer finds least surprising, it picks the most generic ones: distinct-3 falls from 0.749 to 0.699 and cross-sample overlap rises from 0.162 to 0.216, a larger loss of diversity than $\tau{=}0.8$ incurs, and at that diversity the temperature curve is 11.7\% \emph{better} than the selector. In other words, best-of-$N$ by canonicality is a worse version of lowering the temperature. Refining the selected draft recovers part of the loss (best-of-4 + $\RT{6}$: $-25.3\%$ raw, but only $-4.5\%$ against the frontier, versus $-12.4\%$ for $\RT{6}$ alone), and the larger selectors fall below the diversity range of the curve altogether. On LM1B large, where non-canonicality barely varies between drafts, the selector does nothing at all: best-of-4 scores 79.7 against 80.1 for the released sampler and adds 0.8\% to $\RT{6}$ (62.0 vs.\ 62.5). Non-canonicality therefore ranks drafts by quality but is not a free lunch as a filter; regenerating the fine levels on canonical coarse codes is what improves a draft without trading its diversity away.

\paragraph{The re-encoding is what helps.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation} separates the ingredients. The first control asks whether the extra passes alone help. Regenerating levels 6--11 conditioned on the draft's own coarse codes (Resample$_6$) costs the same six passes as $\RT{6}$ but gives no gain at all: 16.43 against 16.15 on TinyStories and $+0.6\%$ on LM1B, both within seed noise. This is expected, since it only draws the fine levels again from the conditional distribution that produced them, and it rules out the simplest explanation, that any second attempt at the fine levels is better than the first. The second control asks whether the text itself matters, or only re-quantization. Re-quantizing the draft's latent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:rq} instead of re-encoding its text (LatentRQ$_6$, a final-latent analogue of image scale-travel, which re-quantizes a partial map at an intermediate scale) changes the coarse codes almost as much, except level 0, which it keeps 72\% of the time, and recovers part of the gain: $-11.5\%$ on TinyStories and $-17.9\%$ on LM1B. But it also lowers diversity, so at matched diversity it is worth $-6.8\%$ and $-7.7\%$, against $-12.4\%$ and $-20.9\%$ for $\RT{6}$. Going through the decoded text, which discards whatever in the latent does not affect the output and lets the encoder describe the text afresh, roughly doubles the matched-diversity gain; the discrete text is a better anchor than the continuous latent. The same ordering holds with two rounds (Resample$_5{\times}2$ $-0.2\%$, LatentRQ$_5{\times}2$ $-19.9\%$, $\RT{5}{\times}2$ $-27.6\%$), so it is not a one-round accident.

\subsection{Spending the gain on diversity}
\label{sec:hot}

\paragraph{Hot drafts.} Because refinement lowers Gen.\ PPL at almost constant diversity, the gain can be spent differently: start from a \emph{hotter} draft, which is more diverse but worse, and let the round trip repair the damage (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:tsfrontier}). A draft at $\tau{=}1.1$ on its own is 16\% worse than the released sampler (18.76), and at $\tau{=}1.2$ 49\% worse (24.01). With the draft at $\tau{=}1.1$ and one round from level 6 at $\tau{=}1$, Gen.\ PPL is 14.15 ($-12.4\%$ against the released sampler, on all seeds) while distinct-3 rises from 0.749 to 0.753, cross-sample overlap falls from 0.162 to 0.156, unigram entropy is unchanged (3.981) and doubled words fall from 0.39\% to 0.26\%. This sampler is better than the released one on every axis we measure, for six extra passes, which no temperature setting can achieve since temperature always trades one axis for the other. With the draft at $\tau{=}1.2$ and the regenerated levels at $\tau{=}0.8$, samples are clearly more diverse than the released sampler's (distinct-3 0.766, overlap 0.143) and still 7.3\% more fluent; the temperature curve at that diversity is at 19.2, so the frontier gain is 22\%, the largest in the paper. Hot drafts with two or three rounds reach $-22\%$ to $-27\%$ Gen.\ PPL at 17--18\% below the frontier. On LM1B large the same recipe works: a $\tau{=}1.2$ draft with $\RT{6}{\times}2$ gives 62.47 ($-22.0\%$) at higher entropy (4.367 vs.\ 4.339), the same distinct-3 (0.894 vs.\ 0.895) and lower cross-sample overlap (0.040 vs.\ 0.043), again better on every axis. The hot-draft settings come from a small grid (draft $\tau \in \{1.1, 1.2\}$, three round schedules) chosen on seeds 1--3 and confirmed on held-out seeds below. Part of these gains comes from the per-level temperature schedule itself rather than from the round trip; Section~\ref{sec:twins} separates the two with exact 11-pass twins.

\subsection{Held-out seeds}
\label{sec:heldout}

\paragraph{Replication with nothing re-tuned.} Every TinyStories setting above (restart level, number of rounds, temperatures) was chosen while looking at seeds 1--3, so the gains reported there could in part be selection effects. Table~\ref{tab:heldout} therefore re-runs the main samplers, the ablations and the full temperature curve on three fresh seeds (4--6, $3\times256$ samples) with nothing re-tuned. Three things should hold if the effect is real, and all three do. First, the raw gains are unchanged: $\RT{6}$ lowers Gen.\ PPL from 16.32 to 13.23 ($-18.9\%$, against $-17.3\%$ on seeds 1--3), and three rounds reach 11.50 ($-29.5\%$). Second, the gain survives the diversity control. Lowering the temperature to $\tau{=}0.8$ also reaches $-17.6\%$, but only by making the samples more alike (distinct-3 falls from 0.748 to 0.717 and cross-sample overlap rises from 0.165 to 0.195); $\RT{6}$ keeps distinct-3 at 0.737 and overlap at 0.173, so at its diversity the temperature curve would give a perplexity 14.5\% higher than $\RT{6}$ achieves. This matched-diversity margin is the quantity that cannot be obtained by turning the temperature knob, and it is the same size as on seeds 1--3 (10--14\%) or slightly larger: 15.3\% for $\RT{5}{\times}2$. The best sampler, a hot $\tau{=}1.2$ draft refined twice with the regenerated levels at $\tau{=}0.8$, is 23.6\% below the released sampler at \emph{the same} diversity (distinct-3 0.742 vs.\ 0.748, overlap 0.165 vs.\ 0.165), which puts it 21.4\% beyond the frontier. Third, the ablation still separates the mechanism from the extra compute: resampling the fine levels without re-encoding changes nothing ($-1.2\%$, within seed noise), so the six additional passes are worthless unless they start from the re-encoded coarse levels. LM1B large behaves the same way: held-out seeds give $-20.2\%$ for $\RT{6}$ and $-27.1\%$ for $\RT{6}{\times}2$, against $-22.0\%$ and $-29.3\%$ on seeds 1--3, a difference of two points that is the expected seed-to-seed variation.

\subsection{Reasoning: Sudoku}
\label{sec:sudoku}

\paragraph{Greedy refinement.} NCM's Sudoku results \citep{kumbong2026ncm} make it a test of whether refinement only polishes surface text or also helps a task with a single correct answer. On the released Sudoku-hard model (1{,}000 test puzzles, greedy decoding as configured), every restart level solves more puzzles than the released sampler (Table~\ref{tab:sudoku}): from 84.1\% to 85.5\% with $\RT{6}$ and 85.9\% with three rounds. These are small differences, so the per-puzzle counts matter more than the percentages: $\RT{6}$ newly solves 14 puzzles and breaks none, three rounds solve 19 and break 1. Because the same 1{,}000 puzzles are scored under both samplers, an exact McNemar test on these counts gives $p{=}1.2\times10^{-4}$ for $\RT{6}$, so a 14-to-0 split is very unlikely to be noise even though 1.4 points is. The gain is small for a reason visible in the codes: here the round trip changes only 2--20\% of the codes per level, far less than the 61--96\% on stories, because a Sudoku board leaves the tokenizer little freedom, and greedy rounds converge quickly ($\RT{6}{\times}2$ and $\RT{6}{\times}4$ are identical). Refinement on this difficulty is therefore a small, essentially regression-free gain, not a large one. It does not generalise across difficulties: on Sudoku-easy there is nothing left to fix (99.6\% to 99.7\%), and on Sudoku-medium it lowers the solve rate from 96.7\% to 95.9\% ($\RT{6}$) and 96.0\% (three rounds), so on that checkpoint the round trip breaks slightly more boards than it repairs.

\paragraph{Verifier-guided search.} Sudoku also has an exact, free verifier (rows, columns, boxes and the given clues), so extra passes can be spent until a board verifies. We compared, at equal mean generator passes per puzzle, two ways of spending that budget: retrying from the current board with a round trip at temperature $T$, or discarding it and drawing a fresh sample. Fresh sampling at $\tau{=}1$ is clearly better at every budget: 89.9\% solved at 16.8 passes per puzzle, 91.0\% at 19.2 and 93.4\% at 23.9, against at most 89.5--90.7\% for round-trip retries at 19--22 passes. The reason is that a round-trip retry keeps most of the wrong board (the coarse plan is re-encoded from it), so repeated retries stay correlated with the same mistake, whereas a fresh sample makes an independent attempt. Resampling only the coarse plan with greedy fine levels does not beat fresh sampling either (best 92.3--93.3\% at about 24 passes over seeds 1, 4, 5 and 6). The practical conclusion: NCM's Sudoku accuracy rises from 84\% to 93\% with verifier-guided test-time compute, but plain resampling, not round-trip refinement, is the way to spend it when an exact verifier exists.

\subsection{Is it a temperature schedule? Exact twins and human text}
\label{sec:twins}

\paragraph{What the twins show.} The twin test asks a sharp question: if we keep every temperature of a recipe but skip the round trip, how much of the gain remains? At the released temperature the twin of $\RT{6}$ is the released sampler itself, so refinement accounts for the whole $-14.5\%$ matched-diversity gain. With hot coarse and cool fine levels the answer changes, and this is the one place where part of our gain is not the round trip: the per-level schedule alone, with no round trip at all, already lies well below the global-temperature curve ($-21.0\%$ for the twin of the best recipe, $-11.3\%$ for the twin of the $\tau{=}1.2$ recipe). Heating the coarse levels and cooling the fine ones is itself a better sampler than a single global temperature, a result not reported in the NCM paper. Refinement adds 4--10 points on top ($-25.2\%$ and $-21.4\%$ against the frontier). We therefore report the hot-draft samplers as ``schedule plus round trip'' and attribute to refinement only its increment. On LM1B large the schedule alone does not help: the twin of the hot-draft sampler is far worse at a similar entropy (90.27 vs.\ 63.37, entropy 4.361 vs.\ 4.368), so there the whole gain is the round trip.

\paragraph{Human text.} The human-text test asks a different question: is the round trip a general ``sharpener'' that makes any text more predictable, or does it specifically repair the generator's drafts? Applied to 768 real human openings instead of drafts, refinement does not lower Gen.\ PPL below that of the human text: one round raises it from 8.51 to 9.39, with distinct-3 and cross-sample overlap essentially unchanged (0.772 vs.\ 0.775, 0.141 vs.\ 0.139), and further rounds raise it more (10.35, 12.12). A sharpening operator would have pushed human text to lower perplexity and lower diversity. Instead the round trip leaves real text close to where it is, and the small rise is the generator's own realisation errors when it regenerates the fine levels. The gain on drafts is therefore a repair of something specific to the drafts, the non-canonical coarse codes, not a bias of the procedure that any input would show.

\subsection{Adaptive rounds: spend passes only where the draft drifts}
\label{sec:adaptive}

\paragraph{Two gates.} Non-canonicality is measured by the same encoder pass that the round trip needs anyway, so it is free to use as a gate. We test two uses of it (Table~\ref{tab:adaptive}, Figure~\ref{fig:adaptive}). \emph{Gated round} ($\ART{k}(\theta)$): run the round from level $k$ only for the samples whose draft the re-encode rewrites more than a share $\theta$ of the level 0--5 codes; the others keep their draft and spend no extra passes. \emph{Early stop} ($\ART{k}{\times}n(\theta)$): repeat up to $n$ rounds, applying the same test to the current text before each round. The reported cost is the mean number of generator passes per sample. The per-round non-canonicality explains why the two gates need different thresholds: drafts are rewritten at a median of 0.76 (TinyStories) and 0.89 (LM1B), but after one round the kept levels \emph{are} the tokenizer's, so the text that comes out is rewritten far less, at a median of 0.44 after $\RT{5}$ and 0.18 after $\RT{6}$ on TinyStories (0.53 after $\RT{6}$ on LM1B), and less again after a second round (0.28 and 0.14). A threshold tuned for drafts therefore never fires a second round; the early-stop thresholds below are set in the range where the second and third rounds actually vary.

\paragraph{What the gates buy.} On TinyStories a gated single round sits slightly below the fixed-schedule curve at every budget: $\ART{6}(0.70)$ refines 86\% of stories for 16.1 passes and reaches 13.61, against 13.84 for $\RT{7}$ at 16 passes; $\ART{6}(0.75)$ refines 59\% for 14.5 passes and reaches 14.19, against 14.76 for $\RT{8}$ at 15; and $\ART{6}(0.80)$ refines only 23\% of stories, for 1.4 extra passes, and matches $\RT{9}$ at 14 passes (15.14 vs.\ 15.13). The gated samplers also keep diversity closer to the released sampler (distinct-3 0.740--0.745), because most stories are left untouched. The same holds against the temperature frontier: at matched distinct-3 the gated rounds are 3--11\% better than temperature, the fixed restarts $\RT{9}$--$\RT{7}$ 4--8\%. Early stop over three rounds behaves the same way: $\ART{6}{\times}3(0.50)$ costs 17.2 passes and scores 13.26 against 13.36 for $\RT{6}$ at 17; $\ART{5}{\times}3(0.50)$ costs 20.8 passes and scores 12.24, better than either fixed two-round schedule at 23 passes (12.34, 12.64) and 14.0\% below the frontier against 10--12\%; above 24 passes the gated samplers are slightly worse in raw perplexity than $\RT{5}{\times}2$ and $\RT{5}{\times}3$ but still equal or better at matched diversity, because the rounds they skip are the ones that would have narrowed the text. On LM1B large the gates are neutral: every draft is rewritten heavily, so the single-round gate at $\theta{=}0.80$ refines 71\% of sequences and lands on the fixed curve ($-15.5\%$ at 15.3 passes against $-16.2\%$ for $\RT{7}$ at 16), and the early-stopped three-round samplers trace the $\RT{6}$--$\RT{6}{\times}2$--$\RT{6}{\times}3$ line within two points. The gates are therefore a way to choose a point on the quality--compute curve per sample rather than per corpus, worth 1--2 passes or 0.3--0.6 Gen.\ PPL on TinyStories, and nothing on LM1B. We report them for completeness and because the selector is free; they do not alter the paper's claims.

\subsection{Wall-clock cost}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{Measured times.} Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports measured times on one RTX 4090 with the authors' batch sizes, split into generator passes and tokenizer work (encoding the decoded text, decoding and token picking). Counting passes understates the cost, because the passes a round repeats are the fine levels, which process the most positions: $\RT{6}$ takes $1.9\times$ the time of the released sampler for $1.55\times$ its passes on both datasets, two rounds $2.8$--$2.9\times$, and three rounds $3.8\times$ on TinyStories. The tokenizer side (re-encoding, decoding, token picking) is about a tenth of the total, so the cost is almost entirely generator passes, and nothing in the method is a hidden bottleneck. The twins of Section~\ref{sec:twins} cost exactly the released sampler's time, which is why we report the hot-draft recipes as schedule plus round trip: the schedule part is free, the round-trip part is not. In absolute terms a refined 128-token story costs 10.5\,ms and an LM1B sequence 65\,ms, against 5.6 and 35\,ms unrefined; the trade is roughly a doubling of sampling time for a 17--22\% perplexity gain, which the reader can weigh against NCM's 10--31$\times$ advantage over diffusion samplers.

\subsection{Does it transfer to images?}
\label{sec:var}

\paragraph{VAR.} Next-scale image models face the same structural situation: VAR \citep{tian2024var} samples a stack of multiscale residual codes and decodes it with a VQ-VAE. We applied the round trip to the released VAR-d16 ImageNet-$256$ model with its FID sampling settings, copying its sampler verbatim and only forcing the coarse scales: decode the draft to an 8-bit image, re-encode it with VAR's own VQ-VAE, keep scales $<k$ and regenerate the rest (Table~\ref{tab:var}; 10 images per class, FID-10k against the ADM reference images with Inception features from \texttt{pytorch-fid}). Forcing every scale to the draft's own codes reproduces all 2{,}000 test drafts exactly. The phenomenon carries over: the image round trip changes 29--92\% of the codes at the first nine scales, the same order as on text, so VAR too samples stacks its tokenizer would not produce. The benefit does not. The best restart moves FID from 9.13 to 8.92, which looks like a gain until the control is read: resampling without re-encoding, which only redraws from the released distribution, already moves FID to 9.22, so differences of 0.1--0.2 are what this 10k-sample measurement produces by chance, and the 8.92 is within that band. Restarting from coarser scales is worse (9.30, 9.35). Two differences from text may explain why canonical coarse codes do not help here: unlike text, the pixel round trip is lossy, so the re-encoded coarse scales describe a slightly different image rather than the same one; and VAR conditions every scale on all earlier scales rather than on the latest reconstruction only, so a corrected coarse scale is only one of many inputs to the fine ones. We did not run the 50k-sample confirmation.

\paragraph{Why distillation fails.} The distillation result deserves a comment, because without diversity metrics it looks like a near-win. The distilled model scores 16.15, exactly the released sampler's number, at the released 11 passes; against its own control (16.78) it is 3.8\% better on every seed, and fine-tuning drift (the control is 3.9\% worse than the released model) seems to be the only obstacle. Corpus diversity tells a different story. The distilled model's distinct-3 is 0.719, the value of the released sampler at $\tau{=}0.8$, and at that diversity the released sampler scores 13.34, not 16.15. Sequence-level distillation \citep{kim2016seqkd} thus transferred the refined sampler's lower diversity but not its fluency at that diversity: at matched distinct-3 the distilled model is about 21\% worse than simply lowering the released model's temperature. In other words, the student learned to be narrower, not better. A larger batch would reduce the drift but not this gap. The common thread of Table~\ref{tab:negative} is that, at this scale, inference-time changes to the released model were far cheaper and more effective than any fine-tune we could afford.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Coarse-to-fine and residual-code generation.} NCM \citep{kumbong2026ncm} brings next-scale prediction \citep{tian2024var,kumbong2025hmar} and residual quantization \citep{lee2022rqvae,borsos2023soundstorm} to text. Infinity's bitwise self-correction \citep{han2024infinity} re-quantizes \emph{training} targets after flipping input bits so that later scales learn to fix earlier errors; our method instead re-quantizes \emph{at inference}, needs no retraining, and goes through the decoded tokens.

\paragraph{Re-quantize and regenerate.} The closest prior method is scale-travel in DiverseVAR \citep{park2025diversevar}: re-quantize the accumulated image latent, rewind to a coarser scale and regenerate, used there to restore quality after diversity-boosting perturbations; no base-sampler result is reported. Round-trip refinement differs in going through the discrete output (decode, then re-encode) rather than re-quantizing the latent, in targeting text, and in being evaluated as a base sampler with frontier controls; LatentRQ$_k$ (Section~\ref{sec:why}) is the direct comparison; on NCM it recovers about half of the matched-diversity gain of the text round trip. SDEdit \citep{meng2021sdedit} similarly re-noises an output and denoises it again with a diffusion model, and Restart sampling \citep{xu2023restart} alternates forward noising and backward sampling to correct accumulated errors, the closest mechanism in continuous diffusion. TTS-VAR \citep{chen2025ttsvar} spends test-time compute on scale-wise image generation through selection.

\paragraph{Iterative refinement and self-correction.} Mask-Predict \citep{ghazvininejad2019maskpredict}, MaskGIT \citep{chang2022maskgit}, Token-Critic \citep{lezama2022tokencritic} and ReMDM remasking \citep{wang2025remdm} revise parallel predictions by re-masking and re-predicting tokens; Self-Refine \citep{madaan2023selfrefine} has large LMs critique and rewrite their own outputs. In NCM, codes within a level are conditionally independent given the coarser levels, so re-masking within a level does not apply; the round trip changes the \emph{conditioning} instead.

\paragraph{Exposure bias.} Training on model outputs \citep{bengio2015scheduled,savinov2021sundae} and student-forcing for scale-wise image models \citep{zhou2025sar} address the train--test gap in training; our own-error corruption experiment is in this family and did not help in fine-tuning at either batch size: at the paper's batch size it reproduces the released model's temperature frontier rather than moving it. Joint or post-training of tokenizers \citep{wang2024larp,leng2025repae,qiu2025tokpost} helps in vision; for NCM text at our scale it tied.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Gen.\ PPL can be lowered by reducing diversity or by low-precision sampling \citep{zheng2024maskedsecret,pynadath2026frontiers,franca2026hacking}; we therefore report temperature frontiers and corpus diversity alongside it.

\section{Limitations}

Our evaluation relies on Gen.\ PPL under GPT-2 Large (checked against Qwen2.5-7B), simple diversity statistics, MAUVE and a single 7B LLM judge with a strong position bias that we average out; we did not run a human evaluation, and at equal Gen.\ PPL the judge does not distinguish refinement from a lower temperature. All models are around 100M parameters with 128-token sequences; the authors list an OpenWebText checkpoint, which would be the only model of long general text, but its weights were not on the hub at the time of writing, so we could not test it. Refinement costs 1.3--2.6$\times$ the generator passes, 1.5--3.3$\times$ the generator FLOPs and 1.9--3.8$\times$ the measured wall-clock, a ratio that compilation and CUDA graphs do not change (Table~\ref{tab:graphs}). It fails when the tokenizer round trip is lossy (WMT14), and it does not transfer to the image model we tried (VAR-d16, FID-10k only). Our reproduction of the released TinyStories sampler is 5\% above the paper's number, and all relative claims are against our reproduction. The train--test mismatch is our explanation of the gain, supported by the canonicality correlations and the ablations but not tested causally; the direct tests, a generator trained on its own sampled stacks, are the self-correction and own-error pilots of Section~\ref{sec:negative}, which both tied with the released model at matched diversity. Restart levels and hot-draft temperatures were chosen on the same three seeds we report, from small grids. Most fine-tuning baselines were limited to batch 64--128, and a 200-step pilot at batch 2{,}048 showed that the control then no longer drifts, so the earlier training-side negative results may partly reflect the small batches, with the exception of the diversity argument against distillation and the two batch-2{,}048 pilots themselves; the own-error result in particular is the same at both batch sizes once diversity is matched.

\section{Conclusion}

NCM's coarse-to-fine sampler produces code stacks that its own tokenizer would never produce for the same text. Passing the draft through the tokenizer and regenerating the fine levels repairs this, with no training and a few lines of code, and lowers generative perplexity by 17--31\% on TinyStories and 22--29\% on LM1B while keeping diversity, 10--14\% beyond what temperature can buy and 15--25\% when paired with a per-level temperature schedule. The ablations locate the gain in the re-encoding itself: resampling without it does nothing, and re-quantizing the latent instead of the text gives about half. The results hold on held-out seeds and do not shrink with model size; exact temperature twins and real human text show that the gain is neither a temperature schedule nor a sharpener; an LLM judge prefers refined stories; MAUVE moves closer to human text; and on Sudoku the gain is limited to the hardest puzzles, while verifier-guided search is better served by plain resampling. The method needs a lossless tokenizer round trip, and it trades extra passes for quality, so it complements rather than replaces NCM's main appeal of speed. Training-time alternatives that we could afford, including distilling the refined sampler, did not help.

\section{Implementation}
\label{app:impl}

Refinement reuses the released \texttt{NextConceptTransformer.generate}. The only change is a wrapper around its per-level sampling function:

\begin{verbatim}
_orig = net._sample_level
def _sample_with_force(logits, temperature, top_k, top_p):
    level = state["calls"] + 1 - state["offset"]   # level-0 queries counted by offset
    state["calls"] += 1
    if state["codes"] is not None and 1 <= level < state["upto"]:
        return state["codes"][level]               # re-encoded codes for levels < k
    return _orig(logits, temperature, top_k, top_p)
net._sample_level = _sample_with_force

draft = generate(l0)                                # authors' sampler, unchanged
codes = split_levels(tokenizer.vqvae.encode(draft)) # re-encode the decoded tokens
refined = generate(codes[0], forced=codes, upto=k)  # regenerate levels k..K-1
\end{verbatim}

All scripts, run logs and per-seed results are in the accompanying repository.
